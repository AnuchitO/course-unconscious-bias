<!--
Facilitator notes — 5 mins.
Affinity bias — you've heard the term "birds of a feather flock together". Affinity bias is our tendency to warm to and hang out with people like ourselves. (Click to fade in definition on the screen.)
Activity: 2–3 mins. The activity is called Circle of Trust. Ask participants to write a list of ten people whom they trust the most and who are not their family members. Read out some diversity dimensions like gender, nationality, native language, accent, age, race/ethnicity, professional background, religion, etc. (maximum 6) and instruct participants to place a tick beside those members who are similar in that dimension to themselves. E.g. you are female and you have a female in your list — place a tick against it. 2 mins only.
End of the exercise — ask participants to reflect on the diversity dimensions and how many members in the list had a lot of ticks. The fewer the ticks, the more diverse the list is.
Share an example of where this may happen in TWU.
-->

# Halo effect

<div class="g-split">
  <div>
    <img class="g-photo" src="/img/halo.jpg" alt="Pink flowers with a glowing halo of light" />
  </div>
  <div>
    <p>Our perception of others creates a <strong>halo above their heads</strong> — often misguided.</p>
    <div class="g-grid-2" style="margin-top: 0.8rem;">
      <div class="g-card accent-green compact"><h3>Halo</h3><p>One positive trait rubs off on the rest.</p></div>
      <div class="g-card accent-red compact"><h3>Horn</h3><p>One negative trait taints the rest.</p></div>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<!--
When we look at the 3 pictures and automatically thought that a person was warm or not warm, that is a Halo effect. The impact: when I feel a certain person is warm, no matter what they do, I might still feel it is right.
Facilitator notes — 5 mins. So now what do you think a Halo effect is?
Halo effect: when one positive attribute or feeling about a person rubs off on other attributes about the person. You can share your own personal example of the Halo effect at TW that you may have demonstrated — alternatively use the one below.
E.g. someone who is always smiling is seen as a 'people' person, an effective team player and a successful professional, and could potentially be rated as exceeding expectations. Someone who is an effective team player is also seen as an effective consultant.
Horn effect (the opposite): e.g. when interviewing someone we might be put off by the fact that they speak very slowly because our unconscious bias has caused us to assume that someone who speaks slowly is unintelligent. E.g. if someone is not fluent in English, because sometimes we equate intelligence and competence with speaking English well. If we assume they're unintelligent, everything they say or do in the term may be clouded by our judgement. They may get fewer chances to speak or facilitate.
-->

<!--
If you tell yourself something 10k times, you will believe it's true.
Facilitator notes — 5–7 mins. Start with the activity:
Tell the group: "We're going to play a game. I'm going to put three numbers on the blank slide using a rule in my head. Your job is to guess the rule. You're going to guess the rule by giving me three numbers, and I will tell you if they do or do not follow the rule."
The rule is "three increasing numbers." Provide the initial three numbers: 2, 4, 6. Ask each participant to send you three numbers along with the rule in the chat. Once everyone has submitted, go through each response in the chat quickly and respond with yes/no on whether it fits the rule.
Tell them the rule in your head and explain the meaning of confirmation bias: the tendency to seek information that confirms preexisting beliefs or assumptions.
Use your own TWU example or the one below.
TWU e.g.: A trainee in T66 had some psychological issues and was undergoing regular counselling. But every time they didn't do something well or behaved unexpectedly, we would become empathetic and assume it was because of their psychological state. # Group think

<div class="g-split">
  <div>
    <img class="g-photo" src="/img/groupthink.jpg" alt="Many hands stacked together" />
  </div>
  <div>
    <p>People try <strong>too hard to fit in</strong> — mimicking others or holding back their own thoughts and opinions.</p>
    <div class="g-callout danger"><p>Teams lose <strong>identity</strong>, creativity and innovation.</p></div>
  </div>
</div>

<!--
This is not a bias. Group think is a phenomenon we need to think about.
Group think occurs when people try too hard to fit into a particular group by mimicking others or holding back thoughts and opinions. E.g. some new hires may not share their viewpoints or solutions during team meetings, as they think it may not be accepted, and hence mimic the rest of the group/team.
This causes them to lose part of their identities and causes organizations to lose out on creativity and innovation.
Ask the group to share their example of group think. Could also be a personal story.
-->

    <h3>2 · At work, they poison</h3>

    <p>Which biases might <strong>I</strong> have?</p>

<!--
1. Please remember biases are not always bad: they can save your life! (Shortcuts)
Imagine this situation: you can smell something burning, you can see smoke, you hear a fire alarm. You are not going to wait and consciously try to find out what is happening. You are going to run and get out of there. If you decide to give up on your fast brain/unconscious bias in this situation, you might just die and get your Darwin award.
2. But it can also be a bad thing! You can share a personal story of an unconscious bias that you discovered, or just talk about it in general. Add examples. So, when we are in a workplace and make decisions about each other, bias can be very poisonous.
3. Ask participants if they are aware of any biases that they may have.
